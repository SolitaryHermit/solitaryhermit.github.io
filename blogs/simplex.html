A Survey on Simplicial Complexes

Throughout this series of notes, we shall always let $R$ be a nonzero commutative ring and $\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$ be the set of all natural numbers (non-negative integers).

Furthermore, given each positive integer $n$, we may denote by $[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}$, and for every (finite) set $X$, we put $2^X:=\mathcal P(X)$ the power set of $X$, i.e., the collection of all subsets of $X$.

Simplicial Complexes

(Simplicial Complexes)

Let $X$ be a finite set. A simplicial complex on the vertex set $X$ is a collection $\Delta\subseteq 2^X$ that is downward-closed, namely for every $F,G\subseteq X$, $$ G\subseteq F~\text{and}~F\in\Delta\implies G\in\Delta $$

  • Each member $F\in\Delta$ is called a face, and a facet if it is maximal there.
  • Dually, every subset of $X$ not contained in $\Delta$ is called a non-face, and a minimal non-face if also minimal among all non-faces.
  • For each face $F\in\Delta$, its dimension is defined as $$ \dim(F):=|F|-1. $$ Faces of dimensions $0$ and $1$ are called vertices and edges, respectively, and $\Delta$ is said to be pure if all of its facets are of the same dimension.
  • In general, the dimension of $\Delta$ is $$ \dim(\Delta):=\max_{F\in\Delta}\dim(F)=\max_{F\in\Delta}|F|-1. $$

Because $|2^X|=2^{|X|}<\infty$, every simplicial complex on $X$ is necessarily finite as well. Furthermore, the dimension of faces is monotone, i.e., for every faces $F,G\in\Delta$, $$ F\subseteq G\implies\dim(F)\le\dim(G). $$ Thus, we essentially have $$ \dim(\Delta)=\max_{F~\text{facet}}\dim(F)=\max_{F~\text{facet}}|F|-1. $$

(Trivial Examples of Simplicial Complexes)
Suppose that $|X|=n$.
  • The complex $\emptyset$ is called the void complex (of dimension $-\infty$).
  • Moreover, if $\Delta\ne\emptyset$ is a simplicial complex, then we necessarily have $\emptyset\in\Delta$, namely $\emptyset$ is always a face of $\Delta$. The simplest nonempty complex $\{\emptyset\}$ is called the irrelevant complex, which is of dimension $-1$.
  • The complex $2^X=\mathcal P(X)$ is also called the full complex, whose dimension is precisely $n-1$.
  • In addition, the boundary is defined as $\partial:=2^X\setminus\{X\}$, which is pure and also of dimension $n-2$.

One can note that a face $F$ is a facet iff every of its superset is a non-face, and oppositely, a non-face $G$ is minimal iff every of its proper subset is a face in $\Delta$.

(Graphs as Simplicial Complexes)

Every simple unordered graph $G=(V,E)$ can be essentially regarded as a simplicial complex on the vertex set $V$: $$ \Delta(G):=\{\emptyset\}\cup\{\{v\}\mid v\in V\}\cup E. $$ Clearly, the facets in $\Delta(G)$ are precisely the edges and $$ \dim \Delta(G)=\begin{cases} 0, & \text{if}~E=\emptyset; \\ 1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$ In fact, every simplicial complex $\Delta$ with dimension at most $1$ can be regarded as a graph. This explains why we call faces of dimensions $0$ and $1$ are called vertices and edges, respectively.

Moreover, the minimal non-faces of $\Delta(G)$ are precisely the non-edges, namely $\{u,v\}\subseteq V$ where $u,v$ are not adjacent in $G$, and also the triangles in $G$:

  • Every singleton in $V$ is contained in $\Delta(G)$, hence always a face.
  • A non-face for $\Delta(G)$ has two elements iff these two elements are not adjacent in $G$. Since every singleton in $V$ is a face, each non-face of size two is necessarily minimal.
  • Note that every subset $S\subseteq V$ of at least three elements is always a non-face for $\Delta(G)$. It is minimal iff all of its subsets are faces in $G$, which forces it to be of size $3$ and all of its pairs to be faces. Therefore, minimal non-faces of size of size at least $3$ are precisely the triangles in $G$.
(Order Complex of Posets)

Let $(P,\le)$ be a finite partially ordered set (poset). All of its chains also form a simplicial complex as well: In fact, we may note that each chain in $(P,\le)$ is a linearly ordered subset, so every of its subset is necessarily linearly ordered as well, namely also a chain in $(P,\le)$.

Therefore, the collection of all chains in $(P,\le)$ is called its associated order complex, denoted by $\Delta(P)$. Clearly, the facets of $\Delta(P)$ are essentially the maximal chains in $(P,\le)$.

(Union & Intersection of Simplicial Complexes)

Given any family $(\Delta_i)_{i\in\Lambda}$ of simplicial complexes on $X$, their intersection $\bigcap_{i\in\Lambda}\Delta_i$ and union $\bigcup_{i\in\Lambda}\Delta_i$ are also simplicial complexes.

Proof

Denote by $\Delta:=\bigcap_{i\in\Lambda}\Delta_i$ and $\Delta':=\bigcup_{i\in\Lambda}\Delta_i$, both of which are clearly collection of subsets of $X$ as well. Now let $F,G\subseteq X$ be arbitrary with $G\subseteq F$.

  • Assume that $F\in\Delta$. Then for each $i\in\Lambda$, we have $F\in\Delta_i$ as well, so by the downward-closedness of $\Delta_i$, it follows that $G\in\Delta_i$. Since $i$ is arbitrary, it follows that $G\in\bigcap_{i\in\Lambda}\Delta_i=\Delta$.
  • Likewise, assume that $F\in\Delta'$. Then there exists $i\in\Lambda$ such that $F\in\Delta_i$, so again, by the downward-closedness of $\Delta_i$, we also have $G\in\Delta_i\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\Lambda}\Delta_i=\Delta'$.

Therefore, we may conclude that both $\Delta$ and $\Delta'$ are simplicial complexes on $X$, as desired. $\blacksquare$

As we shall see then, every simplicial complex is uniquely determined by its facets:
(Simplicial Complexes vs Facets)
  1. Given any simplicial complex $\Delta$ on $X$, if $F_1,\dots,F_m$ are its facets, then $\Delta$ precisely consists of all subsets of them, namely $$ \Delta=\{G\subseteq X \mid \exists i=1,\dots,m:G\subseteq F_i \}. $$
  2. Conversely, for every $F_1,\dots,F_m\subseteq X$, there is a unique smallest simplicial complex $\Delta$ on $X$ containing all of them, which is precisely $$ \Delta=\{G\subseteq X \mid \exists i=1,\dots,m:G\subseteq F_i \}. $$
    • The maximal members of $F_1,\dots,F_m$ are precisely the facets of $\Delta$.
    • All the faces $F_1,\dots,F_m$ are facets of $\Delta$ iff they form an anti-chain in the lattice $(2^X,\subseteq)$, namely no two of them are comparible.
Proof

1. Let $\Delta$ be a simplicial complex on $X$ with facets $F_1,\dots,F_m$. Since $\Delta$ is downward-closed, all subsets of $F_1,\dots,F_m$ necessarily belongs to it. Conversely, fix an arbitrary face $F\in\Delta$. Consider the set $$ \{G\in\Delta\mid G\supseteq F\} $$ of all faces in $\Delta$ containing $F$, which is nonempty as $F$ belongs to it. Furthermore, because $\Delta$ itself is finite, the above set must be finite as well. Consequently, one can find a maximal member $G_0$ there.

We claim that $G_0$ is also a facet in $\Delta$, namely maximal: Given any face $H\in\Delta$ containing $G_0$, we have $F\subseteq G_0\subseteq H$, so $H$ must belong to the set constructed above. By the maximality of $G_0$ there, the equality $G_0=H$ is essentially attained. Therefore, we have $G_0=F_i$ for some $i=1,\dots,m$, and especially $F$ is a subset of $F_i=G_0$, as desired.

2. Fix arbitrary subsets $F_1,\dots,F_m\subseteq X$. Here one may consider the intersection $\Delta$ of all simplicial complexes on $X$ containing them, which is also a simplicial complex by Lemma 2.1. Note that $\Delta$ is a subset of any simplicial complex on $X$ containing $F_1,\dots,F_m$, and in particular, itself is a simplicial complex containing these subset, hence essentially the smallest one there.

Now we turn to the structure of $\Delta$: By the downward-closedness of $\Delta$, we necessarily have $$ \Delta':=\{G\subseteq X \mid \exists i=1,\dots,m:G\subseteq F_i \}\subseteq\Delta. $$ Conversely, it is ready to be seen that $F_1,\dots,F_m$ all belong to the set $\Delta'$. The collection $\Delta'$ is also a simplicial complex on $X$:

  • Each member in $\Delta'$ is a subset of some $F_i$, hence also a subset of $X$.
  • Let $F,G\subseteq X$ with $F\subseteq G$. If $G\in\Delta'$, then $G\subseteq F_i$ for some $i=1,\dots,m$. Now $F\subseteq F_i$ holds as well, hence $F\in\Delta'$.

Therefore, $\Delta'$ is a simplicial complex on $X$ containing $F_1,\dots,F_m$, so the inclusion $\Delta\subseteq\Delta'$ is also trivial. We now precisely have $\Delta=\Delta'$ in this case. What remains is to study the facets of $\Delta$:

  • Let $F\in\Delta$ be a facet. Then $F\subseteq F_i$ for some $i=1,\dots,m$. By the maximality of $F$, we necessarily have $F=F_i$ here, and moreover, if $F_i\subseteq F_j$ for another $j=1,\dots,m$, we must have $F_i=F=F_j$ as well. Thus, we see that $F=F_i$ is also maximal among all of $F_1,\dots,F_m$.
  • The converse is also true: Assume that $F_i$ is maximal among the faces $F_1,\dots,F_m$. If $F_i\subseteq F$ for some face $F\in\Delta$, by definition, we have $F\subseteq F_j$ for some $j=1,\dots,m$ as well. Now $F_i\subseteq F\subseteq F_j$ as well, so by the maximality of $F_i$, it follows that $F_i=F_j=F$. Therefore, the face $F_i$ is precisely a facet in $\Delta$.

Therefore, all faces $F_1,\dots,F_m$ are facets of $\Delta$ iff each of them is maximal among this subfamily, which holds iff no two of them are comparible, i.e., they form an anti-chain in $X$ under inclusion, as desired. $\blacksquare$

(Generated Simplicial Complexes)

For every $F_1,\dots,F_m\subseteq X$, the unique smallest simplicial complex on $X$ containing all of them is called the simplicial complex generated by $F_1,\dots,F_m$, denoted by $\langle F_1,\dots,F_m \rangle$. In particular, every simplicial complex generated by a single subset of $X$ is called a simplex.

(Simplicial Complexes vs Anti-chains)

The assignments $$ \Delta\mapsto\{F\in\Delta\mid F~\text{is a facet}\} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathcal A\mapsto\langle \mathcal A \rangle. $$ are precisely mutually inverse bijections between simplicial complexes on $X$ and anti-chains of $(2^X,\subseteq)$.

Proof

Given any simplicial complex $\Delta$ on $X$, its facets are clearly mutually incomparable, hence form an anti-chain $\mathcal A$ in $2^X$ as well. By Part 1 of Theorem 2.1, we certainly have $\Delta=\langle \mathcal A \rangle$ here.

Conversely, given any anti-chain $\mathcal A$ in $2^X$, by Part 2, itself is precisely the collection of all facets in the generated simplicial complex $\langle \mathcal A \rangle$. The proof is complete. $\blacksquare$

(Alexander Dual)

Let $\Delta$ be a simplicial complex on $X$. Its Alexander dual is defined the following collection of subsets of $X$: $$ \Delta^\vee:=\{F^c\mid F\subseteq X~\text{and}~F\notin\Delta\}, $$ where $F^c:=X\setminus F$ is the complement of $F$ in $X$.

We have also incorporated the function of producing the Alexander dual of simplicial complexes in the interactive widget above. Interested reader may check it out there as well.

(Properties of Alexander Dual)

Let $X$ be a finite set and $\Delta$ be a simplicial complex on $X$.

  1. A subset $F\subseteq X$ is a face in $\Delta^\vee$ iff its complement is a non-face of $\Delta$, namely $$ F\in\Delta^\vee\iff F^c\notin\Delta, $$ so $$ |\Delta^\vee|=2^n-|\Delta|. $$
  2. The Alexander dual $\Delta$ is also a simplicial complex on $X$ with $$ (\Delta^\vee)^\vee=\Delta. $$
  3. The facets (resp. minimal non-faces) in $\Delta^\vee$ are precisely the complements of minimal non-faces (resp. facets) of $\Delta$, so if $\Delta\ne\emptyset$, $$ \dim(\Delta^\vee)=n-1-\min_{G\notin\Delta}|G|. $$
Proof

1. For every subset $F\subseteq X$, $$ F\in\Delta^\vee\iff\exists G\notin\Delta:F=G^c\iff F^c\notin\Delta. $$ Thus, the map $F\mapsto F^c$ is a bijection of $2^X$ mapping $\Delta^\vee$ onto the complement $2^X\setminus\Delta$, so $$ |\Delta^\vee|=|2^X\setminus\Delta|=2^n-|\Delta|. $$

2. Clearly, $\Delta^\vee$ is also a collection of subsets of $X$. Let $F,G\subseteq X$ with $G\subseteq F$. If $F\in\Delta^\vee$, by 1, we see that $F^c\notin\Delta$. Here we have $F^c\subseteq G^c$, so $G^c\notin\Delta$ as well whence $G\in\Delta^\vee$ as well. Therefore, we may conclude that $\Delta$ is a simplicial complex on $X$ as well.

Furthermore, for every $F\subseteq X$, by 1, $$ F\in(\Delta^\vee)^\vee\iff F^c\notin\Delta^\vee\iff F=(F^c)^c\in\Delta, $$ so $\Delta=(\Delta^\vee)^\vee$ holds for sure.

3. By 1, we see that $F\subseteq X$ is a face (resp. non-face) in $\Delta^\vee$ iff its complement is a non-face (resp. face) in $\Delta$. Since the complement of subsets is bijective and reverses the inclusions, it carries the maximal (resp. minimal) elements onto the minimal (resp. maximal) elements. The desired statement is thus trivial for us. Thus, if $\Delta\ne\emptyset$, $$ \begin{aligned} \dim(\Delta^\vee)&=\max_{F\in\Delta^\vee}(|F|-1)=\max_{F^c\notin\Delta}(|F|-1) \\ &=\max_{G\notin\Delta}(|G^c|-1)=\max_{G\notin\Delta}(n-|G|-1) \\ &=n-1-\min_{G\notin\Delta}|G|. \end{aligned} $$ $\blacksquare$

(Trivial Examples of Alexander Duals)
  • The void complex $\emptyset$ and full complex $2^X$ are Alexander duals of each other.
  • The irrelevant complex $\{\emptyset\}$ and the boundary $\partial=2^X\setminus\{X\}$ are also Alexander duals of each other: For every $F\subseteq X$, $$ F\in\{\emptyset\}^\vee\iff F^c\notin\{\emptyset\}\iff F^c\ne\emptyset\iff F\ne X. $$
(Dimension Inequality)

For every simplicial complex $\Delta$ on $X$, $$ n-3\le \dim(\Delta)+\dim(\Delta^\vee)\le 2n-4. $$

Proof

$\blacksquare$

The $f$-Vector of Simplicial Complexes

($f$-Vectors)

Let $X$ be a finite set and $\Delta\ne\emptyset$ be a simplicial complex on $X$ of dimension $d-1\ge 0$. For each $i=-1,0,\dots,d-1$, we may denoted by $$ f_i:=|\{F\in\Delta\mid |F|=i+1\}|, $$ the number of $i$-dimensional faces of $\Delta$. Furthermore, the $d$-tuple $$ f(\Delta):=(f_0,f_1,\dots,f_{d-1})\in\mathbb{N}^d $$ is called the $f$-vector of $\Delta$.

Note that $f_{-1}=1$ since $\emptyset\in\Delta$, which explains why we did not include it in the definition of $f$-vectors.

Given two integers $a,d>0$, we may let $$ a=\binom{k(d)}{d}+\binom{k(d-1)}{d-1}+\dots+\binom{k(j)}{j}, $$ where $$ k(d)>k(d-1)>\dots>k(j)\ge j\ge 1 $$ be the unique $d$-th Macaulay representation of $a$. We shall set $$ a^{(d)}=\binom{k(d)}{d+1}+\dots\binom{k(d-1)}{d}+\dots+\binom{k(j)}{j+1}. $$

(Characterization of $f$-Vectors, Kruskal-Katona)

Let $d\ge 1$ be a positive integer. A vector $(f_0,f_1,\dots,f_{d-1})\in\mathbb{N}^d$ is the $f$-vector of some $(d-1)$-dimensional simplicial complex iff $0 < f_{i+1}\le f_i^{(i+1)}$ for each $i=0,1,\dots,d-2$.

(Skeleta of Simplicial Complexes)

Let $X$ be a finite set and $\Delta\ne\emptyset$ be a simplicial complex on $X$ of dimension $d-1\ge 0$. For each $j\ge -1$, the $j$-th skeleton of $\Delta$ is defined as the collection $\Delta^{(j)}$ of all faces in $\Delta$ of dimension at most $j$, namely $$ \Delta^{(j)}=\{F\in\Delta\mid|F|\le j+1\}. $$

Since the cardinality of sets is monotone respect to set inclusions, one can see that the $j$-skelton $\Delta^{(j)}$ is downward-closed whence also a simplicial complex on $X$.

Clearly, we have $\Delta^{(-1)}=\{\emptyset\}$ and also $\Delta^{(j)}=\Delta$ for all $j\ge d-1$. Furthermore, the inclusion $\Delta^{(j)}\subseteq\Delta^{(j+1)}$ trivially holds for each $j$, hence together, we get a filtration of simplicial complexes on $X$: $$ \{\emptyset\}=\Delta^{(-1)}\subseteq\Delta^{(0)}\subseteq\dots\subseteq\Delta^{(d-1)}=\Delta. $$

Besides, for $-1\le j\le d-1$, it is also immediate for us that $\dim(\Delta^{(j)})=j$ and also $$ f(\Delta^{(j)})=(f_0,f_1,\dots,f_j), $$ which is precisely the truncation of the $f$-vector of $\Delta$. Now also $$ |\Delta^{(j)}|=\sum_{i=-1}^j f_i \quad\text{and}\quad f_j=|\Delta^{(j)}|-|\Delta^{(j-1)}|. $$

($h$-Vectors)

Let $X$ be a finite set and $\Delta\ne\emptyset$ be a simplicial complex on $X$ of dimension $d-1\ge 0$. Then the $h$-vector of $\Delta$ is defined as the unique vector $h(\Delta)=(h_0,h_1,\dots,h_d)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}$ such that $$ \sum_{i=0}^d f_{i-1}(t-1)^{d-i}=\sum_{i=0}^d h_it^{d-i}, $$ where $t$ is an arbitrary indeterminate.

(Relationships Between $f$ and $h$-Vectors)

Let $X$ be a finite set and $\Delta\ne\emptyset$ be a simplicial complex on $X$ of dimension $d-1\ge 0$. For each $k=0,1,\dots,d$, $$ h_k=\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^{k-i}\binom{d-i}{k-i}f_{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{k-1}=\sum_{i=0}^k\binom{d-i}{k-i}h_i. $$

Chain Complexes Associated to Simplicial Complexes

For each $i\in F\subseteq[n]$, we may define $$ \varepsilon(i,F):=(-1)^{\#\{j\in F\mid j< i\}}. $$

(Associated Chain Complexes)

Let $R$ be a ring and $\Delta$ be a simplicial complex on $[n]$.

  • For each $i\in\mathbb{Z}$, if $-1\le i\le n-1$, we let $\tilde{C}_i(\Delta;R)$ be the free $R$-module on symbols $e_F$ with $F\in\Delta$ an $i$-dimensional face; otherwise we set $\tilde{C}_i(\Delta;R)=0$.
  • Furthermore, we may let $\partial_i:\tilde{C}_i(\Delta;R)\to \tilde{C}_{i-1}(\Delta;R)$ be the unique $R$-linear map with $$ \partial_i(e_F):=\sum_{i\in F}(\varepsilon(i,F)\cdot e_{F\setminus\{i\}}), $$ where $F\in\Delta$ is an $i$-dimensional face.
