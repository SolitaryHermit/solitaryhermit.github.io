Monomial Ideals I: Characterizations & Generators

Updated: Sep 9, 2026

Throughout this series of notes, we shall always let $R$ be a nonzero commutative ring, $\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$ be the set of all natural numbers (non-negative integers), and given each positive integer $n$, we may denote by $$ [n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}. $$

Definitions and Characterizations

(Monomial Ideals)

An ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is called a monomial ideal if it is generated by monomials.

Given any $R$-algebra $A$, any of its ideal $I$ is automatically an $R$-submodule: It is certainly an additive subgroup there, while for every $r\in R$ and $x\in I$, we have $$r\cdot x=(r\cdot 1_A)x\in I,$$ where $1_A$ stands for the multiplicative identity in $A$. Because $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is an $R$-algebra (actually the free $R$-algebra on $n$ elements), any ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is necessarily an $R$-submodule.

(Freeness of Monomial Ideals)

Let $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a monomial ideal.

  1. For every polynomial $f\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, we have $$ f\in I\iff\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I. $$ Thus, the ideal $I$ is a free $R$-module in which the monomials form a base over $R$. In particular, $$I\ne R[X_1,\dots,X_n]\implies I\cap R=0.$$
  2. Furthermore, the quotient ring $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I$ is also free over $R$, and the residue classes of all monomials not belonging to $I$ form a base. Consequently, $$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}_R(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I) < \infty&\iff\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq\sqrt{I} \\ &\iff\exists a\in\mathbb{N}:X_1^a,\dots,X_n^a\in I. \end{aligned} $$
Proof

Denote by $\mathcal M:=\{\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}}\mid\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^n\}$ the set of all monomials in $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, and $\mathcal M_I\subseteq\mathcal M$ the subset of monomials in $I$. Note that $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is itself free over $R$ with base $\mathcal M$, so any set of monomials is always $R$-linearly independent.

1. Let $f\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be arbitrary.

  • If $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I$, then $f$ itself, being an $R$-linear combination of monomials in its support, certainly lies in $I$ as well.
  • Conversely, assume that $f\in I$. Because $I$ is generated by monomials, there are monomials $u_1,\dots,u_m\in I$ and also polynomials $f_1,\dots,f_m\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ such that $$f=f_1u_1+\dots+f_mu_m.$$ Thus, we have $$\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(f_1u_1)\cup\dots\cup\operatorname{Supp}(f_mu_m).$$ For each $i=1,\dots,m$, since $u_i\in I$ is a monomial, every member in the support of $f_iu_i$ is necessarily divisible by $u_i$ whence lies in $I$ as well. Thus, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f_iu_i)\subseteq I$ for all $i=1,\dots,m$, so $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I$ holds as well.

The desired equivalent condition is established. Now given any $f\in I$, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I$ whence $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq \mathcal M_I$ as well. This shows that $f$ is always an $R$-linear combination of members in $\mathcal M_I$, so $\mathcal M_I$ generates $I$ as an $R$-submodule of $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$. As noted above, the members in $\mathcal M_I$ are $R$-linearly independent, so $I$ is also free over $R$ with base $\mathcal M_I$.

In addition, given any $c\in I\cap R$, if $c$ is nonzero, we necessarily $1\in I$ whence $I=R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$. Thus, if $I$ is proper, then it follows that $I\cap R=0$ here.

2. Because $I$ is an $R$-submodule of $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, we see that the quotient $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I$ is also generated by the following residue classes as an $R$-module: $$\{\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}}+I\mid\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^n\}=\{0+I\}\cup\{\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}}+I\mid\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}}\notin I\},$$ or equivalently, the residue classes of all monomials not contained in $I$ (namely $\mathcal M\setminus\mathcal M_I$). What remains is to show that these residue classes are $R$-linearly independent: Let $u_1,\dots,u_k\in\mathcal M\setminus\mathcal M_I$ be monomials not in $I$, and $r_1,\dots,r_k\in R$ be such that $$0+I=\sum_{i=1}^k(r_i\cdot(u_i+I))=\sum_{i=1}^k r_iu_i+I.$$ Thus, we have $r_1u_1+\dots+r_ku_k\in I$. Note that each $u_i$ is a monomial, while each $r_i\in R$ is a scalar, so by 1, we have $u_i\in I$ whenever $r_i\ne 0_R$. However, the selection of $u_i$ ensures that $u_i\notin I$ always, so we necessarily have $r_1=\dots=r_k=0_R$. Therefore, we may conclude that $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I$ is also a free $R$-module with the corresponding base.

Finally, we may study the condition for the free rank of $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I$ over $R$ being finite: Since every nonzero commutative ring also has IBN like fields, by our discussions above, we have $\operatorname{rank}_R(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I)<\infty$ iff there are only finitely many monomials not belonging to $I$.

  • First, assume that $\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\not\subseteq\sqrt{I}$. Then there exists some $i$ such that $X_i^k\notin I$ for all $k\ge 1$. Thus, we have an infinite family $\{X_i^k\mid k\ge 1\}$ of monomials not in $I$ in this case.
  • Next, assume that $\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq\sqrt{I}$. For each $i=1,\dots,n$, there exists $k_i\ge 1$ such that $X_i^{k_i}\in I$. Then we may put $a:=\max\{k_1,\dots,k_n\}$ and clearly $X_i^a\in I$ for all $i$ as well since $k_i\le a$.
  • Finally, if there exists some $a$ such that $X_i^a\in I$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$, then any monomial $X_1^{a_1}\cdots X_n^{a_n}\in I$ whenever $a_i\ge a$ for all $i$. Consequently, $$ \{\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\mid \mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\notin I\}\subseteq\{\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\mid\forall i=1,\dots,n:0\le a_i < a \}. $$ Note that the latter set is finite (of $a^n$ elements), so there are indeed finitely many monomials not contained in $I$. $\blacksquare$

Given any field $K$, we know that $\mathfrak m:=\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle$ is a maximal ideal, as $$ K[X_1,\dots,X_n]/\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\cong K $$ is a field. Therefore, if $I\subsetneq K[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is a proper monomial ideal, we see that $$ \begin{aligned} \dim_K(K[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I) < \infty &\iff \mathfrak m\subseteq\sqrt{I}\iff\mathfrak m=\sqrt{I} \\ &\iff I~\text{is $\mathfrak m$-primary}. \end{aligned} $$ A generalization may be found from Corollary 3.1.1 below.

  • It is now immediate for us that $K[X]/(X^2)=K\overline{1}\oplus K\overline{X}$, as $1=X^0,X$ are the only monomials not contained in $(X^2)$. Thus, we have $\dim_K(K[X]/(X^2))=2$.
  • We have $K[X,Y]/(X^2,Y^2)=K\overline{1}\oplus K\overline{X}\oplus K\overline{Y}\oplus K\overline{XY}$, as $1=X^0Y^0,X,Y,XY$ are the only monomials not contained in $(X^2,Y^2)$. Likewise, here $\dim_K(K[X,Y]/(X^2,Y^2))=4$.
  • More generally, we have $$ \dim_K(K[X_1,\dots,X_n]/\langle X_1^{a_1},\dots,X_n^{a_n}\rangle)=a_1\dots a_n, $$ as the monomials not contained in $\langle X_1^{a_1},\dots,X_n^{a_n}\rangle$ are precisely $$ \{\mathbf{X}^{\bf b}\mid\forall i=1,\dots,n:0\le b_i < a_i \} $$ and this set consists of precisely $a_1\dots a_n$ many elements.
(Characterization of Monomial Ideals)

An ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is a monomial ideal iff given any polynomial $f\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, $$f\in I\iff\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I.$$

Proof

One direction is precisely Part 1 of the preceding Theorem 1.1. Conversely, assume such a condition holds for $I$. Suppose that $I=\langle f_i\mid i\in\Lambda\rangle$. Then for each $i$, by hypothesis, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f_i)\subseteq I$ as well. Consequently, we may put $$\mathcal M:=\bigcup_{i\in\Lambda}\operatorname{Supp}(f_i)\subseteq I.$$ It is clear for us that $\langle \mathcal M\rangle\subseteq I$. Conversely, for each $i\in\Lambda$, the polynomial $f_i$ is an $R$-linear combination of monomials in $\operatorname{Supp}(f_i)\subseteq\mathcal M$, hence lies in the ideal generated by $\mathcal M$ as well. Together, we also have $I\subseteq\langle \mathcal M \rangle$, hence the equality follows. Now $I$ is precisely generated by a set of monomials, hence a monomial ideal as well. $\blacksquare$

We may recall that $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is canonically $\mathbb{N}^n$-graded with homogeneous components $R\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}=R(X_1^{a_1}\cdots X_n^{a_n})$ for each $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$. In this case, given any $f=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^n}r_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}$, the $\mathbf{a}$-th homogeneous component of $f$ is essentially $r_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}$ whenever $r_{\mathbf{a}}\ne 0_R$, or equivalently $\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)$.

(Homogeneity of Monomial Ideals)

Let $K$ be a field. An ideal $I\subseteq K[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is a monomial ideal iff it is a homogeneous ideal in $K[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ with respect to the canonical $\mathbb{N}^n$-grading.

Proof

Note from the standard theory of graded rings that $$ \begin{align*} I &\text{ is a homogeneous ideal with respect to the canonical } \mathbb{N}^n\text{-grading} \\ &\iff \forall f \in R[X_1,\dots,X_n] : \left( f = \sum_{\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{N}^n} r_{\mathbf{a}} \mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}} \in I \iff (\forall \mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}} \in \operatorname{Supp}(f) : r_{\mathbf{a}} \mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}} \in I) \right) \\ &\iff \forall f \in R[X_1,\dots,X_n] : \left( f = \sum_{\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{N}^n} r_{\mathbf{a}} \mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}} \in I \iff (\forall \mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}} \in \operatorname{Supp}(f) : \mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}} \in I) \right) \\ &\qquad \text{(since each nonzero } r_{\mathbf{a}} \text{ is a unit)} \\ &\iff \forall f \in R[X_1,\dots,X_n] : (f \in I \iff \operatorname{Supp}(f) \subseteq I) \\ &\iff I \text{ is a monomial ideal.} \end{align*} $$ $\blacksquare$

Dickson's Lemma and Generators of Monomial Ideals

Clearly, given any two monomials $\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}$ and $\mathbf{X}^{\bf b}$ with $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_n),\mathbf{b}=(b_1,\dots,b_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$, we have $$ \mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\mid\mathbf{X}^{\bf b}\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:a_i\le b_i. $$ Thus, the divisibility relation between the monomials is equivalent to the component-wise/product ordering on $\mathbb{N}^n$.

(Dickson's Lemma)

Let $S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^n$ be nonempty.

  1. Every tuple $\mathbf{a}\in S$ is greater than or equal to some member there minimal with respect to the component-wise ordering.
  2. Furthermore, the set $S$ contains only finitely many tuples minimal with respect to the component-wise ordering.
Proof

1. Let $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in S$ be arbitrary. Then there are only $(1+a_1)\dots(1+a_n)$ tuples in $\mathbb{N}^n$ that is less than or equal to $\mathbf{a}$, hence also finitely many tuples in $S$ less than or equal to $\mathbf{a}$. By considering these tuples, one can find a minimal one $\mathbf{a}'$ there.

We claim that $\mathbf{a}'$ is also minimal in $S$: If not, there exists $\mathbf{a}''\in S$ such that $\mathbf{a}''<\mathbf{a}'$ with respect to the component-wise ordering. By the transitivity, it follows that $\mathbf{a}''\le\mathbf{a}$ as well, contradicting to the minimality of $\mathbf{a}'$. The desired stateemnt thus follows.

2. Next, we may proceed by induction on $n$: When $n=1$, the well-ordering principle of $\mathbb{N}$ ensures that there is a unique minimum element in $S$.

Now suppose that $n\ge 2$ and the statement holds for $n-1$. Then we may let $S'\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n-1}$ be the projection of tuples in $S$ onto the first $n-1$ components, namely $$S'=\{(a_1,\dots,a_{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}\mid\exists a_n\in\mathbb{N}:(a_1,\dots,a_{n-1},a_n)\in S\}.$$ By the inductive hypothesis, the set $S'$ contains finitely many members with respect to the component-wise ordering, say $\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_r$. Now by the definition of $S'$, for each $i=1,\dots,r$, there exists $b_i\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $(\mathbf{a}_i,b_i)\in S$. Put $b:=\max\{b_1,\dots,b_r\}$. We then construct the desired subset of $S$ as follows: Fix an arbitrary $(\mathbf{a},d)\in S$ with $\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}$.

  • Suppose that $d\ge b$. Here $\mathbf{a}\in S'$, so there exists $i=1,\dots,r$ such that $\mathbf{a}_i\le\mathbf{a}$ component-wise. Here $b_i\le b\le d$ as well, so $(\mathbf{a}_i,b_i)\le(\mathbf{a},d)$ holds for sure.
  • Otherwise, we may put $$S_d':=\{(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}\mid(a_1,\dots,a_{n-1},d)\in S\},$$ the first $n-1$ components of tuples in $S$ with $d$ the $n$-th coordinate. Again, by the inductive hypothesis, it contains a finite subset $T_d$ consisting of elements minimal with respect to component-wise ordering. In this case, since $\mathbf{a}\in S_d'$, there exists $\mathbf{a}'\in T_d$ such that $\mathbf{a}'\le\mathbf{a}$. Now $(\mathbf{a}',d)\in S$ and also $(\mathbf{a}',d)\le(\mathbf{a},d)$.

Together, we may put $$T:=\{(\mathbf{a}_1,b_r),\dots,(\mathbf{a}_r,b_r)\}\cup\bigcup_{d=0}^{b-1}\{(\mathbf{a}',d)\mid\mathbf{a}'\in T_d\},$$ which, being a union of finite subsets, is also finite. As shown before, every $(\mathbf{a},d)\in S$ is component-wise greater or equal to some tuple in $T$, so by further reduce $T$ if necessary, we can conclude that $S$ contains only finitely many tuples minimal with respect to component-wise ordering, completing the induction. $\blacksquare$

(Generator of Monomial Ideals)

Every monomial ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is always finitely generated. In particular, the following statements are true for us:

  1. Any set $\mathcal M$ of monomials in $I$ generates the ideal iff given any monomial $v\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, $$v\in I\iff\exists u\in\mathcal M:u\mid v.$$
  2. Furthermore, the monomial ideal $I$ always admits a unique minimal system $G$ of monomial generators.
    • The set $G$ coincides with the set of all monomials in $I$ minimal with respect to divisibility, hence also finite.
    • Furthermore, this minimal system $G$ is also contained in every monomial generator of $I$.
Proof

1. Let $\mathcal M\subseteq I$ be a subset of monomials. First, suppose that $I=\langle \mathcal M\rangle$, and let $v\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be an arbitrary monomial.

  • Clearly, if $u\mid v$ for some $u\in\mathcal M$, then we necessarily have $v\in I$.
  • Conversely, assume that $v\in I$. Then there are monomials $u_1,\dots,u_m\in\mathcal M$ and polynomials $f_1,\dots,f_m\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ such that $v=f_1u_1+\dots+f_mu_m$. Since $v$ is a monomial, it follows that $$\{v\}=\operatorname{Supp}(f_1u_1+\dots+f_mu_m)\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(f_1u_1)\cup\dots\cup\operatorname{Supp}(f_mu_m).$$ Thus, there exists some $i$ such that $v\in\operatorname{Supp}(f_iu_i)$. Note that $u_i$ is also a monomial, so every monomial in the support of $f_iu_i$ is divisible by $u_i$. In particular, we have $u_i\mid v$ here as well.

Now conversely, assume that this condition holds for $\mathcal M$. Fix an arbitrary polynomial $f\in I$. Since $I$ is a monomial ideal, by Theorem 1.1, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I$. Write $\operatorname{Supp}(f)=\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ and $f=r_1v_1+\dots+r_kv_k$ for some $r_1,\dots,r_k\in R$. Now for each $i=1,\dots,k$, we have $v_i\in I$, so by hypothesis, there exists $u_i\in\mathcal M$ such that $u_i\mid v_i$. Consequently, we have $v_i=u_iw_i$ for another monomial $w_i\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, so $$f=\sum_{i=1}^k r_iv_i=\sum_{i=1}^k r_i(u_iw_i)=\sum_{i=1}^k(r_iw_i)u_i\in\langle \mathcal M\rangle.$$ Therefore, we indeed have $I=\langle \mathcal M\rangle$ in this case.

2. Denote by $\mathcal M(I)$ the set of all monomials in $I$, and $G\subseteq\mathcal M(I)$ the subset of monomials minimal with respect to divisibility. By Dickson's Lemma, the subset $G$ is finite and also every monomial in $I$ is divisible by some member in $G$. By 1, it is clear that $I=\langle G\rangle$ then, so $I$ is essentially finitely generated. We now turn to the proofs of additional properties of $G$:

  • First, fix an arbitrary monomial generator $\mathcal M$ of $I$. For each $u\in G$, by 1, there exists $v\in\mathcal M$ such that $v\mid u$ as well. Note that $I=\langle G\rangle$ and $G$ also consists of monomials, so by 1 again, one can further find another $u'\in G$ such that $u'\mid v$. Now we have $u'\mid v\mid u$, but $u$ is minimal with respect to divisbility in $\mathcal M(I)$, so necessarily $u=u'=v\in\mathcal M$ here. Thus, we always have $G\subseteq\mathcal M$, and hence every momonomial in $G$ is also minimal with respect to divisibility in $\mathcal M$.
  • Furthermore, assume that $I=\langle G'\rangle$ for some $G'\subseteq G$. Here $G'$ consists of monomials as well, so as shown just before, we necessarily have $G\subseteq G'$ as well. The equality $G=G'$ is thus attained, which implies that the generator $G$ of $I$ is also minimal.
  • Finally, let $\tilde{G}$ be another minimal system of monomial generators of $I$. Since $I$ is finitely generated, the set $\tilde{G}$ must be finite as well. As shown above, we also have $G\subseteq\tilde{G}$, and the converse is also true: For each $v\in\tilde{G}$, ollowing verbatim arguments in the previous items, there are $u\in G$ and $v'\in\tilde{G}$ such that $v'\mid u\mid v$. If $v\ne v'$, then we shall have $$ \langle \tilde{G}\setminus\{v\} \rangle=\langle \tilde{G} \rangle=I, $$ contradicting the minimality of $\tilde{G}$. Therefore, we must have $v=v'=u\in G$, which gives the reversed inclusion $\tilde{G}\subseteq G$. The uniqueness of such a minimal system of monomial generators of $I$ thus follows. $\blacksquare$

The unique minimal set of monomial generators of $I$ is usually denoted by $G(I)$ for convenience. The above theorem also indicates an approach to find $G(I)$ for any monomial ideal $I$: Fix an arbitrary monomial generator $\mathcal M$ of $I$ (better if finite), and then determine its members minimal with respect to divisibility, which are precisely the members of $G(I)$.

If $I=(Y,X^2Z^2,Y^2,YZ^2)$, then $G(I)=\{Y,X^2Z^2\}$.

(ACC on Monomial Ideals)

Every ascending chain of monomial ideals stabilizes.

Proof

Let $(I_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$ be an ascending chain of monomial ideals. Then for $\mathcal M:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}G(I_n)$, by Dickson's Lemma, it has a finite subset $\mathcal M_0$ consisting of all monomials there minimal with respect to divisibility. In this case, there exists $k\in\mathbb N$ such that $$\mathcal M_0\subseteq G(I_1)\cup\dots\cup G(I_k).$$ We claim that $I_n=I_k$ for all $n\ge k$: Given any monomial $u\in G(I_n)$, by the minimality of $\mathcal M_0$, it is divisible by some $v\in\mathcal M_0$, so $u\in\langle \mathcal M_0\rangle\subseteq I_1+\dots+I_k=I_k$. This shows that $I_n\subseteq I_k$, while the converse is trivial by hypothesis. The proof is complete. $\blacksquare$

(Operations of Monomial Ideals)

Let $I,J\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be monomial ideals.

  1. Both $I+J$ and $IJ$ are also monomial ideals with $G(I+J)\subseteq G(I)\cup G(J)$ and $G(IJ)\subseteq G(I)G(J)$.
  2. The intersection $I\cap J$ is also a monomial ideal generated by $\operatorname{lcm}(u,v)$ with $u\in G(I)$ and $v\in G(J)$.
  3. The colon ideal $(I:J)$ is also a monomial ideal with $$(I:J):=\bigcap_{v\in G(J)}(I:v),$$ where each $(I:v)$ is generated by the monomials $u/\gcd(u,v)$ with $u\in G(I)$.
Proof

1. Suppose that $G(I)=\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}$ and $G(J)=\{v_1,\dots,v_s\}$. Then we have $$ I+J=\langle u_1,\dots,u_r,v_1,\dots,v_s \rangle, $$ and $$ IJ=\langle u_iv_j\mid i=1,\dots,r,~j=1,\dots,s\rangle, $$ which immediately tells us that $I+J$ and $IJ$ are both monomial ideals, and the inclusions $G(I+J)\subseteq G(I)\cup G(J)$ and $G(IJ)\subseteq G(I)G(J)$ follow immediately.

2. Given any polynomial $f\in I\cap J$, since $I,J$ are both monomial ideals, by Theorem 1.1, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I$ and $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq J$. Now $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I\cap J$, so we may conclude that $I\cap J$ is also a monomial ideal. Moreover, for each $w\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)$, by Theorem 2.2, there exists $i=1,\dots,r$ and $j=1,\dots,s$ such that $u_i\mid w$ and $v_j\mid w$. In this case, we have $\operatorname{lcm}(u_i,v_j)\mid w$, so $$ w\in\langle \operatorname{lcm}(u_i,v_j)\mid i=1,\dots,r,~j=1,\dots,s \rangle.$$ Since $w\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)$ is arbitrary, it follows that $f\in\langle \operatorname{lcm}(u_i,v_j)\mid i=1,\dots,r,~j=1,\dots,s \rangle$ as well. Note that each $\operatorname{lcm}(u_i,v_j)$ also lies in $I\cap J$, hence $$I\cap J=\langle \operatorname{lcm}(u_i,v_j)\mid i=1,\dots,r,~j=1,\dots,s \rangle.$$

3. Clearly, $$ (I:J)=(I:\langle v_1,\dots,v_s\rangle)=\bigcap_{j=1}^s(I:\langle v_j\rangle)=\bigcap_{j=1}^s(I:v_j).$$ Fix an arbitrary $j=1,\dots,s$. We first check that $(I:v_j)$ is also a monomial ideal:

  • Given any polynomial $f\in(I:v_j)$, we have $fv_j\in I$ whence $\operatorname{Supp}(fv_j)\subseteq I$. In this case, for each $u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)$, we have $uv_j\in\operatorname{Supp}(fv_j)\subseteq I$, so $u\in(I:v_j)$ holds for sure. Therefore, by Theorem 1.1, the ideal $(I:v_j)$ is also a monomial ideal.
  • Furthermore, for each $i=1,\dots,r$, it is clear that $$ \frac{u_i}{\gcd(u_i,v_j)}\cdot v_j=\frac{u_iv_j}{\gcd(u_i,v_j)}=\operatorname{lcm}(u_i,v_j)\in I, $$ as it is certainly divisible by $u_i\in G(I)$. Thus, we have $u_i/\gcd(u_i,v_j)\in(I:v_j)$. Conversely, given each monomial $w\in(I:v_j)$, we have $v_jw\in I$ whence there exists $i=1,\dots,r$ such that $u_i\mid v_jw$. In this case, we see that $(u_i/\gcd(u_i,v_j))\mid w$. By Theorem 2.2, we may conclude that $$(I:v_j)=\langle u_i/\gcd(u_i,v_j)\mid i=1,\dots,r \rangle. $$ $\blacksquare$

For $I=(XY^2,YZ^2)$ and $J=(XYZ,Y^2)$ in $R[X,Y,Z]$, we see that $$ \begin{align*} (I:J)&=((XY^2,YZ^2):XYZ)\cap((XY^2,YZ^2):Y^2) \\ &=(XY^2/XY,YZ^2/YZ)\cap(XY^2/Y^2,YZ^2/Y) \\ &=(Y,Z)\cap(X,Z^2) \\ &=(XY,XZ,YZ^2,Z^2)=(XY,XZ,Z^2). \end{align*} $$

(Colon Ideal)

Let $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a monomial ideal. Then for every nonzero $f\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, the ideal $\bigcap_{u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)}(I:u)$ is the largest monomial ideal contained in $(I:f)$, and $$ (I:f)=\bigcap_{u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)}(I:u)\iff (I:f)~\text{is a monomial ideal}. $$

Proof

Let $0\ne f\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be arbitrary, and denoted by $J_f:=\bigcap_{u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)}(I:u)$.

  • For each polynomial $g\in J_f$, we have $ug\in I$ for every monomial $u\in\operatorname{Supp}(g)$, so $fg\in I$ holds as well, namely $g\in(I:f)$. The inclusion $J_f\subseteq(I:f)$ holds for sure.
  • Note from Theorem 2.3 that $(I:u)$ is also a monomial ideal for each $u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f)$, so is $J_f$. Furthermore, let $v\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a monomial. Then by noting that $I$ is a monomial ideal, $$ \begin{aligned} v\in(I:f)&\iff vf\in I\iff\operatorname{Supp}(vf)\subseteq I \\ &\iff\forall u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f):vu\in I \\ &\iff\forall u\in\operatorname{Supp}(f):v\in(I:u)\iff v\in J_f. \end{aligned} $$ Therefore, the ideal $J_f$ precisely contains all monomials in $(I:f)$, hence essentially the largest monomial ideal contained in it.
  • Clearly, if $(I:f)=J_f$, then $(I:f)$ itself must be a monomial ideal. Conversely, if $(I:f)$ is a monomial ideal, it is precisely generated by all monomials contained in itself, hence $(I:f)\subseteq J_f$ holds as well. The equality thus follows. $\blacksquare$

In general, the equality for $(I:f)=J_f$ is not true. For example, we may consider $I=(X^2,Y^2)\subseteq R[X,Y]$ and $f=X-Y$.

  • Here we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f)=\{X,Y\}$, so $$ \begin{aligned} J_f&=(I:X)\cap(I:Y)=((X^2,Y^2):X)\cap((X^2,Y^2):Y) \\ &=(X,Y^2)\cap(X^2,Y)=(X^2,X^2Y^2,XY,Y^2) \\ &=(X^2,XY,Y^2). \end{aligned} $$
  • Meanwhile, we see that $g:=X+Y\in(I:f)$ as $$ fg=(X-Y)(X+Y)=X^2-Y^2\in(X^2,Y^2)=I. $$ However, $g\notin J_f$ as both monomials in its support are linear, while $J_f$ is generated by monomials of degree $2$.
(Sum & Intersection of Monomial Ideals)

Given any family $(I_\lambda)_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ of monomial ideals in $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, both its intersection $\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{I_\lambda}$ and sum $\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{I_\lambda}$ are also monomial ideals. Furthermore, given another monomial ideal $J$, $$ \left( \bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{I_\lambda} \right)+J=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda} (I_\lambda+J) $$ and $$ \left( \sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}I_\lambda\right)\cap J=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda} (I_\lambda\cap J). $$

Proof

1. Denote by $I:=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{I_\lambda}$. For each $f\in I$, we have $f\in I_\lambda$ for all $\lambda\in\Lambda$. Since $I_\lambda$ is a monomial ideal, by Theorem 1.1, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I_\lambda$. As a result, it follows that $\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq I$, hence $I$ is also a monomial ideal by Theorem 1.1 again.

  • Clearly, for each $\lambda$, we have $I\subseteq I_\lambda$ whence $I+J\subseteq I_\lambda+J$. As a result, it is clear that $I+J\subseteq\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{(I_\lambda+J)}$.
  • Conversely, let $u\in\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{(I_\lambda+J)}$ be an arbitrary monomial. If $u\in J$, then $u\in I+J$ holds for sure. Assume that $u\notin J$ now. For each $\lambda\in\Lambda$, we now have $u\in I_\lambda+J$, while $I_\lambda+J$ is also a monomial ideal generated by the $G(I_\lambda)\cup G(J)$. Thus by Theorem 2.2, $u$ is divisible by one element in $G(I_\lambda)\cup G(J)$. But note that $u\notin J$, so $u$ is essentially divisible by one element in $G(I_\lambda)$ instead, namely $u\in I_\lambda$. In this case, we can see that $u\in I\subseteq I+J$ as well.

2. Likewise, put $I':=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda} I_\lambda$. Then clearly, the union $\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G(I_\lambda)$ of monomial generators also generates $I'$, so $I'$ is essentially generated by monomials as well, namely a monomial ideal.

  • For each $\lambda\in\Lambda$, we have $I_\lambda\subseteq I'$, so $I_\lambda\cap J\subseteq I'\cap J$. Together, it follows that $\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(I_\lambda\cap J)\subseteq I'\cap J$.
  • Conversely, let $u\in I'\cap J$ be an arbitrary monomial. We always have $u\in J$. Meanwhile, beacuse $u\in I'$ and $I'$ is generated by the family $\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G(I_\lambda)$ of monomial ideals, by Theorem 2.2, there exists $v\in\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}G(I_\lambda)$ such that $v\mid u$. Now we can see that $v\in G(I_\lambda)$ for some $\lambda\in\Lambda$, so $u\in I_\lambda$ holds as well. Therefore, we now have $u\in I_\lambda\cap J\subseteq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(I_\lambda\cap J)$. $\blacksquare$

Radical of Monomial Ideals

(Square-Free Monomials)

A monomial $\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}=X_1^{a_1}\cdots X_n^{a_n}$ is called square-free if $a_i\le 1$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$. In general, given any monomial $u=\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}$ with $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$, we define $$\sqrt{u}:=\prod_{i,a_i\ne 0}X_i.$$

For example, for $X^3Y^5Z^2\in R[X,Y,Z]$, we have $$ \sqrt{X^3Y^5Z^2}=XYZ. $$ Likewise, for $X^2Z\in R[X,Y,Z]$, $$ \sqrt{X^2Z}=XZ. $$

For every $n$-tuples $\mathbf{a}:=(a_1,\dots,a_n),\mathbf{b}=(b_1,\dots,b_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$, we may put $$ \mathbf{a}\vee\mathbf{b}:=(\max\{a_1,b_1\},\dots,\max\{a_n,b_n\}) $$ and $$ \mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}:=(\min\{a_1,b_1\},\dots,\min\{a_n,b_n\}). $$ Then for monomials $u:=\mathbf{X}^{\bf a},v:=\mathbf{X}^{\bf b}\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, it is clear for us that $$ \gcd(u,v)=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}} \quad\text{and}\quad \operatorname{lcm}(u,v)=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\vee\mathbf{b}}. $$

For example, for $X^3Y^5Z^2,X^2Y^4Z^3\in R[X,Y,Z]$, we may note that $$ (3,5,2)\vee(2,4,3)=(3,5,3) \quad\text{and}\quad (3.5.2)\wedge(2,4,3)=(2,4,2), $$ so $$ \gcd(X^3Y^5Z^2,X^2Y^4Z^3)=X^2Y^4Z^2 $$ and $$ \operatorname{lcm}(X^3Y^5Z^2,X^2Y^4Z^3)=X^3Y^5Z^3. $$

(Radical of Monomials)

Let $u,v\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be monomials.

  1. If $u=\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}$ for some $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$, then $$ \sqrt{u}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\wedge 1}=\gcd(u,X_1\cdots X_n), $$ where $\mathbf{a}\wedge 1:=(\min\{a_1,1\},\dots,\min\{a_n,1\})$. Thus, $$ \sqrt{u}\mid u \quad~\text{and}~\quad \sqrt{u}=\sqrt{u^k},\quad\forall k\ge 1, $$ and $$ u=\sqrt{u}\iff u~\text{is square-free}. $$
  2. $$ u\mid v\implies\sqrt{u}\mid\sqrt{v}.$$
  3. Furthermore, if $u$ and $v$ are square-free monomials, so are their gcd and lcm.
  4. If $u$ is square-free, then $$ u\mid v\iff\forall k\ge 1:u\mid v^k\iff\exists k\ge 1:u\mid v^k. $$
Proof

1. Suppose that $u=\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}$ for some $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$. For each $i=1,\dots,n$,

  • if $a_i\ge 1$, then the exponent of $X_i$ in $\sqrt{u}$ is precisely $1=\min\{a_i,1\}$;
  • if $a_i=0$, then the exponent of $X_i$ in $\sqrt{u}$ remains to be $0=\min\{a_i,1\}$.

Therefore, we precisely have $$ \sqrt{u}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\wedge 1}=\gcd(\mathbf{X}^{\bf a},\mathbf{X}^1)=\gcd(u,X_1\cdots X_n)\mid u, $$ and hence $$ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{u}=u&\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:a_i=\min\{a_i,1\} \\ &\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:a_i\le 1\iff\text{$u$ is square-free}. \end{aligned} $$

Furthermore, given any positive integer $k$, we claim that $\min\{ka_i,1\}=\min\{a_i,1\}$:

  • If $a_i=0$, then $ka_i=0$ as well, so $$ \min\{ka_i,1\}=\min\{0,1\}=0=\min\{a_i,1\}. $$
  • If $a_i\ge 1$, then $ka_i\ge k\ge 1$ holds for sure, hence $$ \min\{ka_i,1\}=1=\min\{a_i,1\}. $$

As a result, it follows that $$ \begin{aligned} k\mathbf{a}\wedge 1&=(\min\{ka_1,1\},\dots,\min\{ka_n,1\}) \\ &=(\min\{a_1,1\},\dots,\min\{a_n,1\})=\mathbf{a}\wedge 1, \end{aligned} $$ so $$ \sqrt{u^k}=\sqrt{\mathbf{X}^{k\mathbf{a}}}=\mathbf{X}^{k\mathbf{a}\wedge 1}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\wedge 1}=\sqrt{u}. $$

2. Furthermore, write $v=\mathbf{X}^{\bf b}$ with $\mathbf{b}=(b_1,\dots,b_n)\in\mathbb{N}^n$. If $u\mid v$, then $a_i\le b_i$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$. Then for each $i$, we also have $\min\{a_i,1\}\le\min\{b_i,1\}$, so $$ \sqrt{u}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\wedge 1}\mid\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{b}\wedge 1}=\sqrt{v}. $$

3. If $u,v$ are both square-free, then $a_i,b_i\le 1$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$, so $$ \min\{a_i,b_i\}\le a_i,b_i\le\max\{a_i,b_i\}\le 1 $$ holds as well. Consequently, we see that $\gcd(u,v)=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}}$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(u,v)=\mathbf{X}^{\mathbf{a}\vee\mathbf{b}}$ are both square-free as well.

4. Clearly, if $u\mid v$, then for every $k\ge 1$, we have $u\mid v^k$ as well since $v\mid v^k$. Thus, what remains is to show that $u\mid v$ whenever $u\mid v^k$ for some positive integer $k$: If $u\mid v^k$, then we have $a_i\le kb_i$ for each $i=1,\dots,n$. Here each $a_i\in\{0,1\}$ as $u$ is square-free.

  • If $b_i\ge 1$, then $a_i\le b_i$ is always true.
  • Otherwise, we have $b_i=0$ whence $a_i\le k(0)=0$, namely $a_i=0=b_i$ as well.

Together, it follows that $a_i\le b_i$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$, so $u=\mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\mid\mathbf{X}^{\bf b}=v$, as desired. $\blacksquare$

Here we shall introduce the following non-standard notation purely for the convenience of our discussions later: Given any monomial ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, we may put $I^{\rm rad}$ to be the ideal generated by $\sqrt{u}$ for monomials $u\in I$.

(Ideal $I^{\rm rad}$)

Let $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a monomial ideal. Then given any monomial generator $\mathcal M$ of $I$, $$ I^{\rm rad}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in\mathcal M \rangle=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle, $$ which is also the largest monomial ideal contained in $\sqrt{I}$.

Proof

1. Fix an arbitrary monomial ideal $\mathcal M$ of $I$. It is clear that $\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in\mathcal M \rangle\subseteq I^{\rm rad}$. Conversely, fix an arbitrary monomial $v\in I$. By Theorem 2.2, there exists $u\in\mathcal M$ such that $u\mid v$. Consequently, it follows that $\sqrt{u}\mid\sqrt{v}$ by Lemma 3.1, hence $\sqrt{v}\in\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in\mathcal M \rangle$. By the definition of $I^{\rm rad}$, the reverse inclusion $I^{\rm rad}\subseteq\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in\mathcal M \rangle$ holds as well. The desired identity thus follows. In particular, by putting $\mathcal M:=G(I)$, $$ I^{\rm rad}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I) \rangle. $$

2. Furthermore, for every $u=X_{i_1}^{a_1}\dots X_{i_k}^{a_k}\in\mathcal M$ with $i_1,\dots,i_k\in[n]$ and $a_1,\dots,a_k\ge 1$, we have $\sqrt{u}=X_{i_1}\dots X_{i_k}$ here. By putting $a:=\max\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}\ge 1$, it is clear for us that $u\mid(\sqrt{u})^a$, so $(\sqrt{u})^a\in I$ whence $\sqrt{u}\in\sqrt{I}$ as well. This shows that $$ I^{\rm rad}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in\mathcal M\rangle\subseteq\sqrt{I}. $$

Finally, let $J\subseteq\sqrt{I}$ be an arbitrary monomial ideal. Then for each $v\in G(J)$, we have $v\in J\subseteq\sqrt{I}$, so $v^k\in I$ for some positive integer $k$. Note from Lemma 3.1 that $\sqrt{v}=\sqrt{v^k}\in I^{\rm rad}$ and also $\sqrt{v}\mid v$, so $v\in I^{\rm rad}$ holds as well. Together, it follows that $J\subseteq I^{\rm rad}$, hence $I^{\rm rad}$ is the largest monomial ideal in $\sqrt{I}$. $\blacksquare$

(Radical of Monomial Ideals)

Given any monomial ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, we have $$ \sqrt{I}=I^{\rm rad}+\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]), $$ where $\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n])$ is the nilradical of $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$. Thus, if $R$ is reduced, then $\sqrt{I}$ is also a monomial ideal with $$ \sqrt{I}=I^{\rm rad}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I) \rangle. $$

Proof

1. First, assume that $R$ is reduced. We now show that $\sqrt{I}$ is also a monomial ideal: Fix an arbitrary polynomial $f\in\sqrt{I}$, and by Corollary 1.1.1, it suffices to verify if $\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}=\operatorname{Supp}(f)\subseteq\sqrt{I}$ as well. Here we may proceed by induction on $r$:

The base case $r=0$ is trivial as $f=0$ in this case. At the inductive step, we may fix an arbitrary monomial ordering $\le$ on $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, and assume that $u_1$ is maximal among $\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}$ with respect to $\le$. Write $f=a_1u_1+\dots+a_ru_r$ with $a_1,\dots,a_r\in R$ nonzero. Then we have $$ \begin{align*} I\ni f^k&=(a_1u_1+\dots+a_ru_r)^k \\ &=\sum_{\substack{ 0\le i_1,\dots,i_r\le k \\ i_1+\dots+i_r=k }} \frac{k!}{i_1!\dots i_r!}(a_i^{i_1}\dots a_r^{i_r})(u_1^{i_1}\dots u_r^{i_r}). \end{align*} $$

Note that $$ u_1^{i_1}\dots u_r^{i_r}\le u_1^{i_1}\dots u_1^{i_r}=u_1^{i_1+\dots+i_r}=u_1^k, $$ with equality iff $i_2=\dots=i_r=0$. Here since $R$ is reduced, we see that $a_1^k\ne 0_R$. Therefore, we also have $u_1^k\in\operatorname{Supp}(f^k)$ with coefficient precisely $a_1^k$. Since $f^k\in I$ and $I$ is a monomial ideal, it follows that $u_1^k\in I$ whence $u_1\in\sqrt{I}$. Recall that $f\in\sqrt{I}$ as well, so we further have $$ f-a_1u_1=a_2u_2+\dots+a_ru_r\in\sqrt{I}. $$ Here $\operatorname{Supp}(f-a_1u_1)=\{u_2,\dots,u_r\}$ has $r-1$ monomials, so by the inductive hypothesis, it follows that $u_2,\dots,u_r\in\sqrt{I}$. Together, we have $\operatorname{Supp}(f)=\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}\subseteq\sqrt{I}$, completing the induction.

2. We now prove the identity $\sqrt{I}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle=I^{\rm rad}$ under the assumption for $R$ being reduced: It suffices to prove $\subseteq$ here by Lemma 3.2.

Given each monomial $v\in\sqrt{I}$, we have $v^k\in I$ for some positive integer $k$. In this case, by Theorem 2.2, there exists $u\in G(I)$ such that $u\mid v^k$. Since $\sqrt{u}\mid u$ always, we now have $\sqrt{u}\mid v^k$. Here $\sqrt{u}$ is square-free, so by Lemma 1.1, we also have $\sqrt{u}\mid v$ here. This shows that $v\in\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle$. Since $\sqrt{I}$ is also a monomial ideal by 1, it follows that $$ \sqrt{I}\subseteq\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle, $$ as desired.

3. In general, we may put $R_{\rm red}:=R/\operatorname{nil}(R)$ and consider the canonical ring homomorphism $\pi:R[X_1,\dots,X_n]\twoheadrightarrow R_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ induced from the quotient map $R\twoheadrightarrow R_{\rm red}$, whose kernel is precisely $$ \operatorname{nil}(R)R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n])\subseteq\sqrt{I}. $$ Here $R_{\rm red}$ is itself reduced, so is the polynomial ring $R_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$. Note that $\pi$ is also surjective, so $$ I':=IR_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]=\pi(I), $$ which is also a monomial ideal as the ring homomorphism $\pi$ preserves the monomials. In particular, $$ I'=IR_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]=\langle G(I)\rangle_{R_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]}, $$ so by 2 and surjectivity of $\pi$, we have $$ \sqrt{I'}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I) \rangle_{R_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]}=I^{\rm rad}R_{\rm red}[X_1,\dots,X_n]=\pi(I^{\rm rad}). $$ Note that $$ \begin{aligned} \pi^{-1}(\sqrt{I'})&=\sqrt{\pi^{-1}(I')}=\sqrt{\pi^{-1}(\pi(I))} \\ &=\sqrt{I+\ker(\pi)}=\sqrt{I+\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n])}\overset{*}{=}\sqrt{I}, \end{aligned} $$ where $\overset{*}{=}$ follows from the fact that $\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n])\subseteq\sqrt{I}$. Therefore, $$ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{I}&=\pi^{-1}(\sqrt{I'})=\pi^{-1}(\pi(I^{\rm rad})) \\ &=I^{\rm rad}+\ker(\pi)=I^{\rm rad}+\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]). \end{aligned} $$ The proof is complete. $\blacksquare$

Let $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a monomial ideal. Then $\operatorname{rank}_R(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I) < \infty$ iff $G(I)$ contains a pure power of each variable $X_i$.

Proof

By Theorem 1.1 and the preceding Theorem 3.1, we see that $$ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{rank}_R(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I) < \infty \\ {}\iff{}& \langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq\sqrt{I}=I^{\rm rad}+\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]). \end{aligned} $$ We claim that the latter condition is equivalent to $\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq I^{\rm rad}$: One direction here is trivial. Conversely, assume that $\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq\sqrt{I}$. Then for each $i=1,\dots,n$, we have $$ X_i=f+g,\quad\text{where}~f\in I^{\rm rad},~g\in\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]). $$ Since $X_i$ is itself a monomial, we may replace $f,g$ with their corresponding terms in $X_i$, namely there are $a,b\in R$ with $$ X_i=aX_i+bX_i,\quad\text{where}~aX_i\in I^{\rm rad},~bX_i\in\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]). $$ As we can see, if $X_i\notin I^{\rm rad}$, since $I^{\rm rad}$ is a monomial ideal, we see from Theorem 1.1 that $aX_i\notin I^{\rm rad}$ whenever $a\ne 0_R$. Consequently, we necessarily have $a=0_R$ here whence $$ X_i=bX_i\in\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]), $$ which is impossible. Therefore, we must have $X_i\in I^{\rm rad}$ here, hence $\langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq I^{\rm rad}$.

Finally, note from Lemma 3.2 that $I^{\rm rad}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle$ is a monomial ideal, so by Theorem 2.2, $$ \begin{aligned} \langle X_1,\dots,X_n\rangle\subseteq I^{\rm rad}&\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:X_i\in I^{\rm rad}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle \\ &\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:\exists u\in G(I):\sqrt{u}\mid X_i \\ &\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:\exists u\in G(I):\sqrt{u}=X_i \\ &\iff\forall i=1,\dots,n:\exists a_i\ge 1:X_i^{a_i}\in G(I). \end{aligned} $$ In conclusion, we see that $\operatorname{rank}_R(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/I) < \infty$ iff $G(I)$ contains a pure power of each $X_i$, as desired. $\blacksquare$

(Square-Free Monomial Ideals)

A monomial ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is called square-free if it is generated by square-free monomials.

In particular, if a monomial ideal $I$ is square-free, then $G(I)$ consists of square-free monomials as well, as it is contained in any generator of $I$ formed by square-free monomials (cf. 2.2).

(Radical Monomial Ideals)

Let $I\subsetneq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a proper monomial ideal.

  1. The radical $\sqrt{I}$ is also a monomial ideal iff $R$ is reduced.
  2. Consequently, the monomial ideal $I$ is radical iff itself is square-free and also $R$ is reduced.
Proof

1. One direction is clear from the preceding Theorem 3.1. Conversely, we may note that $\sqrt{I}$ is also proper in $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ as $I$ is. If $\sqrt{I}$ is also a monomial ideal, then by Theorem 1.1, we also have $$ 0=\sqrt{I}\cap R\supseteq\operatorname{nil}(R), $$ where $\operatorname{nil}(R)$ is the nilradical of $R$. Therefore, we have $\operatorname{nil}(R)=0$, namely the ring $R$ is necessarily reduced here.

2. If $I$ is radical, then $\sqrt{I}=I$ is also a monomial ideal. By 1, the ring $R$ is reduced and especially by Theorem 3.1, we have $$ I=\sqrt{I}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle, $$ which is precisely generated by square-free monomials.

Conversely, assume that $R$ is reduced and $I$ is square-free. Then every member in $G(I)$, as noted above, is also square-free, so by Theorem 1.1 again, $$ \sqrt{I}=\langle \sqrt{u}\mid u\in G(I)\rangle=\langle u\mid u\in G(I)\rangle=I, $$ implying that $I$ is also radical. $\blacksquare$

Integral Closure of Monomial Ideals

(Monomials in Integral Closures)

Let $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be a monomial ideal. Given any monomial $u\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, we have $u\in\overline{I}$ iff there exists a positive integer $k$ such that $u^k\in I^k$.

Proof

Let $u\in R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=:P$. Clearly, if $u^k\in I^k$ for some positive integer $k$, then $$ T-u^k=T+0T^{k-1}+\dots+0T-u^k\in P[T] $$ is an equation of integral dependence of $u$ over $I$, so $u\in\overline{I}$ holds for sure.

Conversely, assume that $u\in\overline{I}$. Then there are $m\ge 1$ and $f_k\in I^k$ for each $k=1,\dots,m$ such that $$ u^m+f_1u^{m-1}+\dots+f_{m-1}u+f_m=0. $$ For each $k=1,\dots,m$, we may let $a_k\in R$ be the coefficient of $u^k$ in $f_k$. In this case, the coefficient of the monomial $u^m$ on the LHS is $1_R+a_1+\dots+a_{m-1}+a_m$, so by the identity there, it follows that $$ 1_R+a_1+\dots+a_{m-1}+a_m=0_R. $$ As a result, at least one of $a_i$'s is nonzero, so we have $u^k\in\operatorname{Supp}(f_i)$. Since $f_k\in I^k$ and moreover $I^k$ is also a monomial ideal (cf. 2.3), it follows from Theorem 1.1 that $u^k\in I^k$, as desired. $\blacksquare$

(Newton Polyhedron)

For every monomial ideal $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, its Newton polyhedron is defined as the convex hull of the exponents of all monomials belonging to $I$ inside the $n$-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$, namely $$ \mathcal C(I):=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^n\mid \mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\in I\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n. $$

(Integral Closure of Monomial Ideals)

Let $I\subseteq R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be monomial ideal. Then $$ \overline{I}=\langle \mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\mid\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal C(I) \rangle+\operatorname{nil}(R[X_1,\dots,X_n]). $$ Thus, if $R$ is reduced, then $\overline{I}$ is also a monomial ideal with $$ \overline{I}=\langle \mathbf{X}^{\bf a}\mid\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal C(I) \rangle. $$

Proof

$\blacksquare$
